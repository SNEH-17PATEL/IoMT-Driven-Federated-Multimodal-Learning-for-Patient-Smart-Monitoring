## How to Read This Document

Every AI system that helps make a medical decision has to answer one question: **"How do we know it is right?"**

ClinIQ produces two things for every patient reading:

1. **A SOFA score.** A number from 0 to 24, predicted by our neural network.
2. **An AI clinical report.** A written assessment generated by a large language model (LLM).

These two outputs go wrong in very different ways, so each has its own checks:

| Output | How it usually goes wrong | How we catch it |
|---|---|---|
| SOFA number | It is off by a few points, or it misses a very sick patient | Statistics on held-out patients, plus "is this physically possible?" checks, plus an uncertainty range |
| Written report | It sounds confident but invents a number, ignores a dangerous vital, or suggests a harmful drug | Nine independent checks that compare the text against the real patient data and medical rules |

Every section below follows the same pattern:
- **In one line:** the idea in plain words.
- **How it works:** the actual rule or formula, taken from the code.
- **Example:** a worked case with real numbers.
- **Why it matters:** what would go wrong without it.

All the numbers in this document (thresholds, weights, scores) are the ones the running code uses. The file each one comes from is listed in [Section 18](#18-where-each-check-lives-in-the-code).

# Part 1 — SOFA Score Validation

---

## 1. What the SOFA Score Is

**In one line:** SOFA is a 0–24 score that measures how badly a patient's organs are failing. Higher means sicker.

**SOFA** stands for **Sequential Organ Failure Assessment**. ICU doctors around the world use it. It looks at **six organ systems** and gives each one 0 to 4 points:

| Organ system | What doctors measure | 0 points (healthy) | 4 points (critical) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory** (lungs) | Blood oxygen vs. oxygen given (PaO₂/FiO₂) | above 400 | below 100, on a ventilator |
| **Coagulation** (blood clotting) | Platelet count | above 150,000 /µL | below 20,000 /µL |
| **Hepatic** (liver) | Bilirubin in the blood | below 1.2 mg/dL | above 12.0 mg/dL |
| **Cardiovascular** (heart and blood pressure) | Mean arterial pressure (MAP) and blood-pressure drugs | MAP ≥ 70, no drugs | high doses of drugs needed |
| **Central nervous system** (brain) | Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) | 15 (fully alert) | below 6 (almost unresponsive) |
| **Renal** (kidneys) | Creatinine and urine output | creatinine below 1.2 mg/dL | creatinine above 5.0 mg/dL |

**Total SOFA = the six scores added together.** The minimum is 0 and the maximum is 6 × 4 = 24.

**Example:**
```
A patient has:
  Lungs     → 2 points (needs extra oxygen)
  Clotting  → 0 points
  Liver     → 1 point
  Heart     → 3 points (on a blood-pressure drug)
  Brain     → 1 point (slightly confused)
  Kidneys   → 2 points
  ───────────────────
  SOFA = 9  → Moderate Risk, close to High
```

**ClinIQ's risk bands** (used everywhere on the dashboard):

| Predicted SOFA | Risk band | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| below 5 | Low Risk | green |
| 5 to below 10 | Moderate Risk | amber |
| 10 or more | High Risk | red |

**Why it matters:** a higher SOFA is strongly linked to a higher chance of death. A single number that summarises organ failure lets a nurse or doctor see quickly who needs attention first.

## 2. Why Our Model Cannot Be Perfect

**In one line:** a true SOFA score needs blood tests, and ClinIQ does not have them, so the model has to estimate SOFA from indirect clues.

A real SOFA calculation needs **lab results** (blood gases, platelets, bilirubin, creatinine). ClinIQ works in real time from things that do not need a needle:
- bedside monitor vitals (heart rate, oxygen, blood pressure, and so on)
- a webcam (eye opening and facial stress)
- the words in the clinical notes

| SOFA part | What it really needs | What ClinIQ has instead | How good is our clue? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lungs | Blood gas (PaO₂/FiO₂) | SpO₂ from a finger clip | Indirect: SpO₂ stops changing once oxygen is high |
| Clotting | Platelet count | Words in notes like "plt" or "thrombocytopenia" | Indirect: only if the doctor wrote it |
| Liver | Bilirubin | Words like "bilirubin" or "jaundice" | Indirect: often not written |
| Heart | MAP and drug doses | MAP (measured directly) plus notes | Good |
| Brain | Full GCS (eye, voice and movement) | Eye opening only (webcam) | Partial: 1 of 3 parts |
| Kidneys | Creatinine and urine | Words like "creatinine" or "oliguria" | Indirect |

**An everyday analogy:** imagine guessing someone's exam mark from their attendance and how tired they look, without seeing their answers. You can tell a strong student from a struggling one, but you cannot guess 72 vs. 75 precisely.

That is exactly the pattern in our results (Section 3). The model is good at telling **sick from not sick**, but weaker at the exact number **inside** a risk band.

## 3. Layer 1 — Offline Statistical Metrics

**In one line:** after training, we test the model on patients it has never seen and measure how close its guesses are.

### When and how this runs

These numbers are calculated **once, at the end of training** (`train_federated.py`), on a **held-out test set**: 20% of the data, set aside before training starts. The model never sees these patients while learning, so this works like a final exam with new questions.

The results are saved to `models/training_metadata.json`. The backend sends this file with every prediction (as `_training_meta`), and Layer 1 ("Population Stats") of the dashboard's **SOFA Validation** drawer (`SOFAValidationDrawer.jsx`) reads **every number live from it**: AUROC, PR-AUC, MAE, R², the alert recall, specificity, missed cases and false alarms at the live threshold, the 3-band confusion matrix (and the 3-band accuracy computed from it), and the sample counts. Nothing is hard-coded any more, so after retraining the drawer updates by itself. If the metadata is missing, it shows "—". (An earlier version of the drawer had fixed numbers typed in, historical: 0.9461 / 0.6840 / 1.8968 / 0.5995 / 2,559 rows / 19.8% / 56.9%.)

**Current results** (from the latest training run, test set of **10,450 ICU stays**, of which 602 are High Risk):

| Metric | Value | One-line meaning |
|---|---|---|
| MAE | **1.82** SOFA points | The average guess is about 1.8 points off |
| R² | **0.47** | The model explains about 47% of why patients differ |
| AUROC (High Risk) | **0.925** | Ranks a High Risk patient above a non-High Risk one 92.5% of the time |
| PR-AUC (High Risk) | **0.646** | Finds the rare sick patients far better than chance (chance ≈ 0.06) |
| Alert recall (pred ≥ 7) | **67.1%** | Of the truly High Risk patients, the alert caught 67% (404 of 602) |
| Alert specificity (pred ≥ 7) | **96.3%** | Of the patients who were not High Risk, 96.3% got no false alarm |

> **Note:** the model was trained with **federated learning**: 3 simulated hospitals, 100 rounds, FedAvg + FedProx, plain MSE loss, network 108 → 256 → 128 → 64 → 1 with dropout 0.3. The best round was round 26. The numbers above belong to that best model. How and why the training changed is explained in `docs/TRAIN_FEDERATED.md`, Section 15.

### 3.1 MAE — Mean Absolute Error

**In one line:** on average, how many SOFA points the guess is off by.

**How it works:**
```
For every patient:  error = | predicted SOFA − actual SOFA |
MAE = the average of all these errors
```
(The `| |` bars mean "ignore the minus sign": being 2 too high and being 2 too low both count as 2.)

**Example with 4 patients:**
```
Patient A: actual 3,  predicted 4    → error 1
Patient B: actual 8,  predicted 7    → error 1
Patient C: actual 12, predicted 10   → error 2
Patient D: actual 6,  predicted 9    → error 3
MAE = (1 + 1 + 2 + 3) / 4 = 1.75
```

**Our result: MAE = 1.82.** On a 0–24 scale, being about 2 points off is roughly 8% of the scale. By band: Low 1.33, Moderate 2.48, High 4.33. The error is biggest for the sickest patients, because the model under-predicts them. Because the risk bands are 5 points wide, a 2-point error usually keeps the patient in the right band, but not always (see 3.5).

**Why MAE and not MSE (mean *squared* error)?** MSE squares each error, so one big miss of 6 counts as 36. MAE treats it as 6. MAE is also what a doctor understands without explanation: "we are about 2 points off on average."

---

### 3.2 R² — How much of the difference between patients we explain

**In one line:** R² compares our model with the laziest possible guess, "everyone gets the average SOFA".

**How it works:**
```
R² = 1 − (our total squared error) / (squared error of always guessing the average)
```
- **R² = 1.0** → perfect predictions.
- **R² = 0.0** → no better than guessing the average for everyone.
- **R² below 0** → worse than guessing the average.

**Example:**
```
Actual SOFA for 3 patients: 2, 6, 10      → average = 6
Lazy guess "6, 6, 6":  squared errors = 16 + 0 + 16 = 32
Our model "3, 6, 9":   squared errors =  1 + 0 +  1 =  2
R² = 1 − 2/32 = 0.94   (we explain 94% of the differences)
```

**Our result: R² = 0.47.** The model explains about 47% of the differences in SOFA between patients, using no blood tests at all. (For comparison, a centralized gradient-boosting model on the same inputs reaches about 0.49, so this is close to what these inputs allow.)

#### R² inside each risk band can be negative, and that is expected

If you only look at, say, the Low Risk patients (SOFA 0–4), the R² inside that group can be **below zero**. That sounds alarming, but here is why it happens:

```
Inside the Low Risk band, real scores are 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4.
What separates a 1 from a 3 here is mostly clotting, liver and kidney
— exactly the parts we cannot measure without blood tests.

So the model correctly says "this patient is Low Risk" (that is what
keeps the overall R² positive), but it cannot reliably tell 1 from 3.
Inside such a narrow band, "always guess the band average" is very hard to
beat, which pushes the within-band R² below zero.
```

**The takeaway:** the overall R² shows the model separates sick from healthy patients. The within-band R² shows it cannot fine-rank patients inside a band without lab data. Both are honest and expected.

---

### 3.3 AUROC — Does the model rank sick patients above others?

**In one line:** pick one truly High Risk patient and one who is not. AUROC is the chance the model gives the sick one the higher score.

**How it works:**
- "Positive" means actual SOFA ≥ 10 (truly High Risk).
- The score used is the **raw predicted SOFA** (for example 7.4 or 11.2), not the yes/no alert.
- AUROC checks every possible cut-off line at once.

**Interpreting it:**
- **1.0** → always ranks the sick patient higher (perfect).
- **0.5** → a coin flip.
- **Our 0.925** → in about 92.5 out of 100 random pairs, the sicker patient gets the higher predicted SOFA.

**Example:**
```
Truly High Risk patient → model predicts 9.1
Not High Risk patient   → model predicts 4.3
9.1 > 4.3  → ranked correctly ✓
Repeat for every possible pair → the fraction ranked correctly = AUROC
```

**Why use the raw number and not the alert?** Where to draw the alert line is a policy choice. AUROC measures whether the model's **ordering** is right, whatever line you pick.

**Why AUROC alone is not enough:** only about 6% of test patients are High Risk (602 of 10,450). When the sick group is that small, AUROC can look good even if the model is poor at actually finding them. That is why we also report PR-AUC.

---

### 3.4 PR-AUC — How well do we find the rare sick patients?

**In one line:** PR-AUC measures how many sick patients we catch, and how many of our alarms are real.

Two key words:
- **Precision:** of the patients we flagged, how many were really sick? ("Are our alarms trustworthy?")
- **Recall:** of the patients who were really sick, how many did we flag? ("Did we miss anyone?")

**Example:**
```
100 patients, of whom 10 are truly High Risk.
The model flags 8 patients; 6 of them are truly High Risk.

Precision = 6 / 8  = 0.75   (75% of alarms were real)
Recall    = 6 / 10 = 0.60   (we caught 6 of the 10 sick patients)
```

PR-AUC summarises this trade-off across every possible cut-off.

**Our result: PR-AUC = 0.646.** The baseline to compare against is the share of sick patients, about **0.06**: a model guessing randomly would score about 0.06. Scoring 0.646 means the model genuinely finds High Risk patients rather than just predicting "not sick" for everyone.

### 3.5 The 3-band confusion matrix

**In one line:** a table of "what band did we predict" vs. "what band was it really".

**Our actual results** (10,450 test ICU stays; rows = actual band, columns = predicted band):

| Actual ↓ / Predicted → | Low | Moderate | High | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** | **6,617** ✓ | 305 | 7 | 6,929 |
| **Moderate** | 2,159 | **669** ✓ | 91 | 2,919 |
| **High** | 112 | 203 | **287** ✓ | 602 |

The diagonal (bold) holds the correct predictions: 6,617 + 669 + 287 = 7,573 of 10,450, a **3-band accuracy of 72.5%**. How to read the rest:
- **Low patients:** 6,617 of 6,929 (95.5%) were put in the right band.
- **Moderate patients:** only 669 of 2,919 (22.9%) were right; most mistakes (2,159) went down to Low.
- **High patients:** 287 of 602 (47.7%) were *predicted* as High; 203 were predicted as Moderate and 112 as Low.

**Why this table matters even with a good MAE:**
```
A patient with actual SOFA 10 is predicted as 8.
Error = 2 points (looks fine for MAE)
But the band changes from High to Moderate.
```
The table shows exactly how often that kind of band-crossing happens. The most dangerous cells are **actual High predicted as Low or Moderate** (112 + 203 = 315 patients).

**Per-band scores** (what the drawer shows):

| Band | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0.74 | 0.95 | 0.84 |
| Moderate | 0.57 | 0.23 | 0.33 |
| High | 0.75 | 0.48 | 0.58 |

**Reading the High row:** when the model *does* predict High, it is usually right (precision 0.75). But it predicts High for fewer than half of the truly High patients (recall 0.48), because it **under-predicts** severe cases: for truly High Risk patients the average prediction is only about 9.7, just under the band line of 10. This is the main reason for the lower alert threshold in the next section.

**Reading the Moderate row:** Moderate is the hardest band. Most Moderate patients are predicted as Low, because what lifts a patient from 3–4 to 5–9 is often a lab value (platelets, bilirubin, creatinine) that the model cannot see.

### 3.6 The alert confusion matrix (does the red alert fire correctly?)

**In one line:** the dashboard raises an alert when **predicted SOFA ≥ 7**; this table checks those alerts against patients whose **actual SOFA ≥ 10**.

The threshold is one constant, `ALERT_THRESHOLD = 7.0`, defined in `model_utils.py`. The backend (`backend/services/ml_service.py`) imports it and `compute_sofa_risk_metrics()` uses it by default, so the dashboard and the training report always use the same line. The alert controls the red alert banner, the `is_alert` flag, and the urgent ("immediate actions first") AI report format in both Hospital and Home Care modes.

**The four possible outcomes** (10,450 test ICU stays):

| | Truly High Risk (actual ≥ 10) | Not High Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Alert fired** (pred ≥ 7) | ✅ True positive: **404** | ⚠️ False alarm: **360** |
| **No alert** | ❌ Missed: **198** | ✅ True negative: **9,488** |

```
Recall (sick patients caught)     = 404 / (404 + 198)     = 67.1%
Specificity (healthy left alone)  = 9,488 / (9,488 + 360) = 96.3%
Precision (alarms that were real) = 404 / (404 + 360)     = 52.9%
F1 (balance of the two)           = 0.59
```

#### Why the alert fires at 7 and not 10

The model tends to **guess too low for severe patients** (average prediction for truly High Risk patients ≈ 9.7). If the alert waited for a prediction of 10, it would fire for only the 47.7% of sick patients the model predicts as High (Section 3.5). Lowering the line catches many of the patients the model under-scored.

**Why 7 and not 8 (the earlier value)?** Until 2026-11-12 the line was at 8. Moving it to 7 was a trade:
```
Alert at 8 → missed 234 High Risk patients, 220 false alarms   (recall 61.1%, specificity 97.8%)
Alert at 7 → missed 198 High Risk patients, 360 false alarms   (recall 67.1%, specificity 96.3%)
→ 36 more sick patients caught, for 140 more false alarms
```

The full sweep on the current model:

| Alert when predicted SOFA ≥ | Recall | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 61.1% | 97.8% |
| 7.5 | 64.3% | 97.1% |
| **7 (current)** | **67.1%** | **96.3%** |
| 6.5 | 69.9% | 95.3% |
| 6 | 73.1% | 93.9% |

In an ICU, **missing a dying patient is far worse than an extra alarm**, so we accept more false alarms to catch more sick patients. Below 7 the false alarms grow quickly for small gains, so 7 is the balance point. The threshold changes only the yes/no alert; AUROC, PR-AUC and MAE stay the same.

**The honest number to remember:** even at 7, **198 truly High Risk patients (33%) got no alert.** This is why ClinIQ is a decision-support tool, not a replacement for clinical judgement.

## 4. Layer 2 — Runtime Plausibility Checks

**In one line:** every time a new prediction is made, three quick checks ask, "Is this number even possible given the vitals we just measured?"

Layer 1 tells us how good the model is **on average**. It says nothing about **this patient, right now**. We cannot know the true SOFA at the bedside, but we can still spot predictions that are impossible.

**Analogy:** if a thermometer in a warm room reads −50 °C, you do not need statistics to know it is broken. These checks apply the same logic to SOFA.

**When they run:** on **every prediction**, which happens **every 90 seconds** while a patient's dashboard is open (`run_prediction()` in `ml_service.py`). The results appear in the **SOFA Validation** drawer. They **flag** a problem; they never change the predicted number.

---

### 4.1 Check 1 — The physiological "floor"

**In one line:** some vitals guarantee a minimum SOFA. If the prediction is below that minimum, something is wrong.

Three things we measure directly each map to a SOFA organ: MAP (heart), SpO₂ (lungs) and GCS Eye (brain). If they are abnormal, the SOFA **must** be at least a certain value.

**The floor rules** (`compute_sofa_floor`):

| If… | Add to the floor | Why |
|---|---|---|
| MAP < 70 mmHg | +1 | Low blood pressure → heart score is at least 1 |
| MAP < 65 mmHg | +1 more (total +2) | Even lower → blood-pressure drugs usually needed |
| SpO₂ < 94% | +1 | Low oxygen → lung score is at least 1 |
| SpO₂ < 90% | +1 more (total +2) | Severely low oxygen |
| GCS Eye = 2 (opens only to pain) | +2 | Poor responsiveness → brain score is at least 2 |
| GCS Eye = 1 (never opens) | +3 | Unresponsive → brain score is at least 3 |

**Example:**
```
Vitals: MAP = 61, SpO₂ = 89%, GCS Eye = 4 (eyes open normally)

  MAP < 70   → +1   (floor = 1)
  MAP < 65   → +1   (floor = 2)
  SpO₂ < 94  → +1   (floor = 3)
  SpO₂ < 90  → +1   (floor = 4)
  GCS Eye 4  → +0
  ──────────────────
  Floor = 4

Model predicts 2.1 → below the floor → FLAGGED
  "Prediction may be underestimated — these vitals imply a SOFA of at least 4."
Model predicts 5.0 → above the floor → OK
```

**Why it matters:** these vitals cover only 3 of the 6 organs, so the floor is a **minimum**, never a full SOFA. A prediction below it is very likely too low, which is exactly the dangerous direction for a model that already tends to under-predict.

---

### 4.2 Check 2 — Dangerous combinations of vitals

**In one line:** some **pairs** of abnormal vitals mean a serious condition, even if neither looks extreme alone.

**The two rules** (`vital_consistency_flags`):

**Rule A — shock pattern**
```
IF heart rate > 130  AND  systolic BP < 90  AND  predicted SOFA < 4
→ FLAG: "HR > 130 + SBP < 90 implies shock state — SOFA expected ≥ 4"
```
A racing heart together with low blood pressure is the classic sign of **shock**: the heart is working hard because blood pressure is failing.

```
Example: HR = 142, SBP = 82, model predicts 3.0 → FLAGGED
```

**Rule B — two organs failing together**
```
IF SpO₂ < 88%  AND  MAP < 65  AND  predicted SOFA < 6
→ FLAG: "SpO₂ < 88% + MAP < 65 implies multi-organ stress — SOFA expected ≥ 6"
```
Very low oxygen **and** very low blood pressure at the same time means at least two organs are in trouble.

```
Example: SpO₂ = 85%, MAP = 58, model predicts 4.5 → FLAGGED
```

---

### 4.3 Check 3 — Sudden jumps with no reason

**In one line:** SOFA should not leap by more than 4 points between readings if the vitals barely changed.

**How it works** (`check_trajectory`):
```
1. Look up the previous prediction for this patient.
2. jump = current SOFA − previous SOFA
3. Count how many vitals changed by a meaningful amount:
     heart rate      by 20 bpm or more
     resp. rate      by 4 breaths/min or more
     SpO₂            by 5% or more
     systolic BP     by 20 mmHg or more
     MAP             by 15 mmHg or more
4. If the jump is bigger than 4 points AND no vital changed meaningfully
   → FLAG it as an unexplained jump.
```

**Example:**
```
10:00:00  SOFA 6.0  | HR 88  RR 18  SpO₂ 94  SBP 112  MAP 78
10:01:30  SOFA 11.0 | HR 90  RR 19  SpO₂ 93  SBP 115  MAP 80

jump = +5.0, but every vital moved only slightly
→ FLAGGED: "SOFA ↑ 5.0 pts (6.0 → 11.0) with no significant vital sign change"
```

**What it protects against:**
- the model behaving unstably on almost identical inputs
- a data error sending a wrong reading
- a change in the note text swinging the prediction while the patient is unchanged

The first reading for a patient is never flagged, because there is nothing to compare it with.

## 5. Layer 3 — Conformal Prediction Interval

**In one line:** instead of a single number, we show a range that contains the true SOFA about 90% of the time.

### The problem with one number

"SOFA = 7" alone hides how sure the model is. Could the truth be 6–8, or 3–11? A doctor needs to know.

Saying "7 ± MAE" (7 ± 1.8) is misleading: MAE is an average, and many patients have errors much bigger than 1.8.

### How the range is built

1. Take the patients in the 3 hospitals' training data.
2. Ask the model to predict each one and record the size of each error.
3. Sort the errors from smallest to largest.
4. Find the error that **90% of patients are at or below**. Call it **q̂** (say "q-hat").
5. For any new patient: **range = prediction − q̂ to prediction + q̂**, kept within 0–24.

**Example:**
```
Errors (sorted, simplified): 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.1, 1.4, 1.9, 2.4, 3.1, 3.9, 6.0
90% of them are at or below 3.9  → q̂ = 3.9

New patient: model predicts 7.0
Range = 7.0 − 3.9 to 7.0 + 3.9 = 3.1 to 10.9
```

**Our value:** with the current model, **q̂ = 3.34** (computed when the backend starts, from the training data). A prediction of 7.0 is shown as roughly **3.7 to 10.3**. On the dashboard it is the **"Conformal prediction interval"** bar under the SOFA gauge. If the training data is unavailable, the code falls back to **q̂ = 2.9**.

**Why the range is useful:**
```
"±MAE" says:     "most people are about 1.8 off, so you probably are too"
Conformal says:  "90% of past patients were within 3.34 — here is that range"
```
A wide range is a signal for caution: the model is telling you it is not sure.

### An honest caveat

The textbook 90% guarantee needs q̂ to be measured on **separate calibration data the model did not train on**. Our q̂ is measured on the training patients, where the model's errors are smaller than on new patients. So the real coverage on new patients is likely **a little under 90%**. A clean fix is to hold back a separate calibration set when training. This is listed in [Section 17](#17-known-limitations).

## 6. Why a Language Model Needs Different Checks

**In one line:** an LLM can write a report that sounds perfect but is wrong, so we check it against the real data, not against itself.

A number can be compared with the right number. A paragraph cannot be checked that easily. LLMs can produce **fluent, confident, medical-sounding text** that is factually wrong. This is called **hallucination**.

**A dangerous example:**
```
Real vitals:  SpO₂ = 84%,  MAP = 52 mmHg   (both critically low)

AI report:    "Oxygen saturation is 96%, within the acceptable range.
               Blood pressure is stable. Continue routine monitoring."

It reads professionally, and a busy clinician might trust it.
It is completely wrong and could cost the patient's life.
```

### The approach we replaced

The first version asked the LLM the same question **three times** and checked whether the answers agreed. The idea was "if it says the same thing three times, it is probably right." That is false:

```
Real vitals: SpO₂ = 84%, MAP = 52 (critical)

Answer 1: "Patient is stable. Monitor vital signs."
Answer 2: "Patient is stable. Continue current management."
Answer 3: "Patient appears stable. No intervention required."

Agreement score: 0.94 → would have been labelled "High Reliability"
Truth: all three are dangerously wrong.
```

Agreement measures **consistency**, not **correctness**. A model can be consistently wrong.

### What we do now

We use **9 methods that each check the report against something external**: the actual vitals, ICU treatment rules, and the model's own reasoning. Two kinds:

| Type | Methods | Extra AI calls | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rule-based (deterministic)** | Methods 1–7 | none | instant |
| **AI judge** | Methods 8–9 | 1 extra call | a few seconds |

---

## 7. How a Report Is Generated and Scored

Here is everything that happens, in order, when the dashboard shows an AI report:

```
1. Every 90 s the dashboard asks the backend for a new prediction.
   The backend predicts SOFA and remembers the full result for this patient.

2. The dashboard asks for a report, sending ONLY the patient ID and care mode
   (Hospital or Home Care). The backend uses its own saved prediction,
   never data sent by the browser.

3. Cache check: if an identical report (same patient, same care mode, same
   vitals) was made in the last 90 s, it is returned instantly.

4. Call 1 — the report: the model `openai/gpt-oss-120b` (via Groq) writes the
   4-section report. Temperature 0.15 (low, so it is factual and consistent).

5. Methods 1–7 run instantly on the text.

6. Call 2 — the judge: the SAME model reads the report and grades it
   (Methods 8–9). It has 12 seconds; if it is late, those two methods
   score a neutral 0.5.

7. The 9 scores are combined into one reliability score (0–1) with a label
   (High / Moderate / Low) and a list of specific problems found.

8. The dashboard shows the report, the score ring, and (in the
   LLM Validation drawer) the per-method breakdown and the problems found.
```

**The four sections every report must have:**

| Section | Hospital mode | Home Care mode |
|---|---|---|
| CURRENT CONDITION | Organ-by-organ findings, with values | Plain-English findings |
| PROBABLE CAUSE | The likely mechanism, citing measured values | A simple explanation for family and carers |
| RISK FORECAST | What may get worse, with thresholds and timeframes | Both possible outcomes: worse (go to hospital) and better (stay home) |
| IMMEDIATE ACTIONS | Treatments grouped by organ system | Practical steps for a home nurse or family |

---

## 8. Method 1 — Factual Grounding

**Weight: 13%** · **In one line:** for every vital that is abnormal, did the report even mention it?

**How it works** (`factual_grounding_score`): for each abnormal vital, the report must contain at least one related word (matching ignores upper/lower case):

| If… | The report must mention one of… |
|---|---|
| SpO₂ < 90% | hypox, oxygen, o2, spo2, saturation, fio2, ventilat, respiratory, breathing |
| MAP < 65 | hypotension, vasopressor, fluid, resuscitat, pressure, map, pressor, norepinephrine, dopamine |
| HR > 100 | tachycardia, heart rate, hr, pulse, cardiac |
| RR > 20 | tachypnea, respiratory, breathing, rr, breath, ventilat |
| SOFA ≥ 10 | high, severe, critical, emergent, immediate, urgent |
| SOFA 5 – <10 | moderate, significant, concerning, monitor, attention |

(Word parts such as "hypox" also match "hypoxia" and "hypoxemia".)

```
Score = (abnormal items mentioned) / (abnormal items present)
If nothing is abnormal → score = 1.0
```

**Example:**
```
Patient: SpO₂ 86%, MAP 58, HR 118 → 3 abnormal items (SOFA 4, so no severity item)

Good report: "...respiratory compromise and hypotension... oxygen and
              vasopressor support... heart rate is elevated..."
  SpO₂ → "respiratory", "oxygen" ✓    MAP → "hypotension" ✓    HR → "heart rate" ✓
  Score = 3/3 = 1.00 ✅

Bad report: "The patient requires close observation."
  none of the required words → Score = 0/3 = 0.00 ❌
```

**Why it matters:** this is the most basic test. A report that does not notice the problem cannot help treat it.

---

## 9. Method 2 — Clinical Hard Rules

**Weight: 13%** · **In one line:** if a dangerous condition is present, did the report recommend the standard treatment?

Method 1 asks "did you **notice** the problem?" Method 2 asks "did you **recommend the right action**?" The rules come from international ICU guidelines (Surviving Sepsis Campaign, ACLS).

**The 6 rules** (`_LLM_HARD_RULES`):

| # | When the rule applies | The report must mention one of… |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | SpO₂ < 90% | oxygen, ventilat, intubat, fio2, high-flow, supplemental, o2, respiratory support |
| 2 | MAP < 65 | vasopressor, norepinephrine, epinephrine, dopamine, phenylephrine, fluid, resuscitat, hypotension, pressor |
| 3 | HR > 130 **and** SBP < 90 | shock, fluid, vasopressor, resuscit, hemodynamic, pressor |
| 4 | SOFA ≥ 10 | immediate, urgent, critical, emergent, priority, severe |
| 5 | Stress Score ≥ 6.5 (High) | pain, sedation, agitation, comfort, distress, analgesia |
| 6 | GCS Eye = 1 | consciousness, gcs, neurological, unresponsive, coma, glasgow |

```
Score = 1 − (rules broken) / (rules that applied)
If no rule applies → score = 1.0
Every broken rule is listed for the clinician.
```

**Example (the "three wrong answers" case from Section 6):**
```
Patient: SpO₂ 84%, MAP 52 → rules 1 and 2 apply

Report: "The patient is stable. Continue monitoring and reassess in one hour."
  Rule 1: no oxygen word        → BROKEN
  Rule 2: no vasopressor/fluid  → BROKEN
  Score = 1 − 2/2 = 0.00 ❌

Shown to the clinician:
  ⚠ SpO₂ < 90% → must mention oxygen therapy
  ⚠ MAP < 65 mmHg → must mention vasopressors or fluid resuscitation
```

**Why it matters:** this check does not care how polished the writing is. If the patient needs oxygen and the report does not say so, the report fails.

---

## 10. Method 3 — SHAP–LLM Coherence

**Weight: 9%** · **In one line:** does the report talk about the same things that actually drove the model's SOFA prediction?

**SHAP** is a method that tells us **which inputs pushed this patient's SOFA up or down the most**. For example: "low SpO₂ added +2.1 points, low MAP added +1.8."

This check makes sure the **report and the model agree on what is wrong**. If the model's main reason was low oxygen but the report never mentions oxygen, the two are telling different stories.

**How it works** (`shap_coherence_score`):
- Take the model's **top 5 SHAP features** for this reading.
- Ignore any whose effect is tiny (less than 0.1 SOFA points).
- For each remaining feature, the report must mention a related word:

| Top feature | Related words |
|---|---|
| latest_SpO2 / SpO2_mean / SpO2_min | spo2, oxygen, hypox, saturation (plus respiratory, o2 for the latest value) |
| latest_MAP / MAP_mean | map, blood pressure, hypotension (plus vasopressor, pressor for the latest value) |
| latest_HR | heart rate, hr, tachycardia, pulse, cardiac |
| latest_RR | respiratory, breathing, tachypnea, rr, breath |
| GCS_eye_opening | gcs, consciousness, neurological, glasgow |
| stress_score | stress, pain, agitation, distress, comfort |
| any other feature (for example a note word like "sepsis") | the feature name itself |

```
Score = (top features the report addresses) / (top features checked)
If no SHAP data is available → 0.5 (neutral)
```

**Example:**
```
Top features: SpO2_mean (+2.1), MAP_mean (+1.8), latest_HR (+0.9)

Report: "respiratory distress with an elevated heart rate; start oxygen"
  SpO2_mean → "oxygen"           ✓
  MAP_mean  → no BP words        ✗
  latest_HR → "heart rate"       ✓
  Score = 2/3 = 0.67
```

**Why it matters:** this is the only check that links the **neural network's reasoning** to the **language model's explanation**.

---

## 11. Method 4 — Response Structure

**Weight: 9%** · **In one line:** does the report have all 4 required sections and enough detail?

**How it works** (`response_structure_score`):
```
Section part = (how many of the 4 headings appear) / 4
Length part  = min(1, word count / 200)       ← 200 words counts as "enough"

Score = Section part × 0.70 + Length part × 0.30
```
The headings it looks for are "current condition", "probable cause", "risk forecast" and "immediate action".

**Examples:**
```
All 4 sections, 350 words:  1.00 × 0.70 + 1.00 × 0.30 = 1.00 ✅
2 sections,      80 words:  0.50 × 0.70 + 0.40 × 0.30 = 0.47 ❌
4 sections,      60 words:  1.00 × 0.70 + 0.30 × 0.30 = 0.79  (headings but little substance)
```

**Why it matters:** a report with only "Current Condition" and no "Immediate Actions" leaves the doctor without guidance. One line per heading is not useful either, which is why length is part of the score.

---

## 12. Method 5 — Severity Calibration

**Weight: 8%** · **In one line:** does the report's tone match how sick the patient really is?

Two failures to catch:
- **Under-alarming:** a critical patient gets calm "keep monitoring" language, so treatment is delayed.
- **Over-alarming:** a stable patient gets panic language, so staff start ignoring alarms (alarm fatigue).

**How it works** (`severity_calibration_score`): the report is scanned for two kinds of words:
```
Urgent words: immediate, urgent, critical, emergent, emergenc(y), severe,
              life-threatening, danger
Calm words:   stable, monitor, reassess, routine, continue, improve,
              recovering, adequate
```

| Patient's SOFA | Rule | Score |
|---|---|---|
| 10 or more (High) | Must contain an urgent word | 1.0 if yes, **0.2** if no |
| 5 – <10 (Moderate) | Either tone is acceptable | 1.0 |
| below 5 (Low) | Urgent words **with no calm words at all** = over-alarming | **0.4** if so, otherwise 1.0 |

**Examples:**
```
SOFA 13: "Continue monitoring and reassess hourly."              → no urgent word → 0.2 ❌
SOFA 2:  "Immediate intervention required — critical emergency." → urgent, no calm → 0.4 ❌
SOFA 11: "Requires urgent vasopressor support and immediate review." → 1.0 ✅
```

---

## 13. Method 6 — Contraindication Safety

**Weight: 5%** · **In one line:** did the report recommend a drug that is dangerous for this patient's current vitals?

This is **all-or-nothing**: any dangerous drug → **0.0**; none → **1.0**.

**The 3 rules** (`_CONTRA_RULES`):

**Rule 1 — No beta-blockers in shock**
```
When:      MAP < 65 AND HR > 100
Forbidden: beta-blocker, metoprolol, atenolol, carvedilol, propranolol, labetalol
Why:       In shock, the fast heart rate is the body compensating for low blood
           pressure. Slowing the heart can make the shock worse.
```

**Rule 2 — No breathing-suppressing drugs when oxygen is very low**
```
When:      SpO₂ < 90%
Forbidden: morphine, opioid, benzodiazepine, midazolam, lorazepam
Why:       These drugs reduce the urge to breathe; with oxygen already
           critically low, that can stop breathing altogether.
```

**Rule 3 — No NSAID painkillers in ICU patients (always applies)**
```
Forbidden: nsaid, ibuprofen, diclofenac
Why:       NSAIDs reduce blood flow to the kidneys and raise bleeding risk;
           ICU patients' kidneys are already under stress.
```

**Example:**
```
Patient: MAP 58, HR 118  → Rule 1 applies
Report: "Consider metoprolol to control the heart rate…"
→ "metoprolol" found → Score = 0.0 ❌
Shown: "Beta-blockers contraindicated in cardiogenic shock (MAP<65 + HR>100)"
```

**The AI judge backs this up:** if the judge (Section 15) decides any recommendation is dangerous, even one not on these lists, this score is forced to **0.0** and the judge's explanation is shown.

**Why the weight is only 5%:** most reports trigger none of these rules, so the check usually scores 1.0. When it does fire, the clinician still sees the specific warning, whatever the overall score.

---

## 14. Method 7 — Numeric Accuracy

**Weight: 8%** · **In one line:** every number the report quotes about the patient must match a real value.

Method 1 would pass "SpO₂ is 96%" just because it mentions SpO₂, even if the real value is 84%. Method 7 checks the **numbers themselves**.

**How it works** (`numeric_hallucination_score`):

**Step 1 — find the numbers that describe the patient.** Two patterns:
```
A number next to a vital's name:  "heart rate of 118", "SpO2 84", "SOFA score 9.2"
A number with a medical unit:     "52 mmHg", "118 bpm", "84%", "38.2 °C"
```

**Step 2 — decide whether each number is allowed.** A number is fine if **either**:
- **it is close to a real value:** within **10% of the real value, or within 3 units, whichever is larger**. The real values checked are HR, RR, SpO₂, temperature, SBP, DBP, MAP, GCS Eye, stress and SOFA.
- **it is a standard clinical reference number**, such as 65, 90, 100 or 37.5. Reports often quote targets like "keep MAP above 65" or normal ranges like "95–100%", which are not patient values.

**Step 3 — score:**
```
Score = max(0, 1 − 0.25 × number of unsupported numbers)
0 wrong → 1.00 · 1 → 0.75 · 2 → 0.50 · 3 → 0.25 · 4+ → 0.00
Up to 3 wrong numbers are listed for the clinician.
```

**Example:**
```
Real: SpO₂ 84%, MAP 52, HR 70, RR 16, SBP 110, DBP 50, Temp 37, SOFA 6

Report: "SpO₂ reads 96% and MAP is 81 mmHg. Target MAP above 65."
  96 → nearest real value is 110 (SBP): |96 − 110| = 14, allowed = 11 → WRONG ✗
       and 84 (SpO₂): |96 − 84| = 12, allowed = 8.4 → still WRONG ✗
  81 → near 84 (SpO₂): |81 − 84| = 3, allowed = 8.4 → passes
  65 → standard clinical target → allowed ✓
  Score = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75
```

**An honest limitation, visible in this example:** the check compares a number with **any** real value, not only the vital it is attached to. So "MAP is 81" passed because 81 happens to be close to the SpO₂ of 84, not the MAP of 52. The judge (Method 9) is meant to catch statement-level mistakes like this one. A stricter version would match each number to its own vital.

---

## 15. Methods 8 and 9 — The AI Judge

**Weights: G-Eval 13%, RAGAS 22%** · **In one line:** a second AI call reads the report like a senior doctor would and grades it.

### Why we need it

Keyword rules are strict. They can miss correct medical wording:
```
Rule 1 needs a word like "oxygen" for SpO₂ < 90%.
A report writing "Start supplemental O2 via high-flow nasal cannula"
passes only because "o2" and "high-flow" happen to be on the list.
A phrase like "increase the inspired fraction" would be missed entirely.
```
An AI judge understands meaning, not just exact words.

### How the judge works (`call_judge_llm`)

- **Model:** `openai/gpt-oss-120b` on Groq, the same model that writes the report. Temperature 0 (as deterministic as possible).
- **What it is given:** the vitals (SpO₂, MAP, HR, RR, SOFA) and the **first 900 characters** of the report.
- **What it must return:** a small JSON object:
```json
{
  "factual_accuracy": 4,
  "clinical_appropriateness": 5,
  "urgency_calibration": 4,
  "completeness": 3,
  "unsupported_claims": ["Blood pressure is within normal limits"],
  "dangerous_recommendation_detected": false,
  "dangerous_details": ""
}
```
- **Time limit:** 12 seconds. If the judge is late or fails, Methods 8 and 9 each score a **neutral 0.5** and the report is still shown.

### Method 8 — G-Eval (overall clinical quality)

The judge scores 4 aspects from **1 (poor) to 5 (excellent)**:

| Aspect | Question the judge answers |
|---|---|
| Factual accuracy | Are the vitals described correctly? |
| Clinical appropriateness | Are the recommendations standard ICU care? |
| Urgency calibration | Does the tone match the severity? |
| Completeness | Are the sections present and substantial? |

```
G-Eval = (average of the 4 scores − 1) / 4      → turns 1–5 into 0–1
Example: 4, 5, 4, 3 → average 4.0 → (4.0 − 1) / 4 = 0.75
```

### Method 9 — RAGAS Faithfulness (claim-by-claim truth check)

The judge lists every statement in the report that is **not supported** by the real vitals.
```
RAGAS = max(0, 1 − 0.30 × number of unsupported claims)
0 → 1.00 · 1 → 0.70 · 2 → 0.40 · 3 → 0.10 · 4+ → 0.00
```

**Example:**
```
Real: MAP 52, SpO₂ 89%
"Blood pressure is within normal limits"  → unsupported ✗
"Oxygen saturation is acceptable"          → unsupported ✗
RAGAS = 1 − 2 × 0.30 = 0.40
```

**Safety override:** if the judge sets `dangerous_recommendation_detected = true`, Method 6 is forced to 0.0 and the judge's explanation appears in the list of problems.

### Honest notes about the judge

- **It grades its own work.** The same model writes and grades the report, and models tend to rate their own output favourably. The 7 rule-based methods are the independent part of the score.
- **It only sees the first 900 characters**, which usually covers the first one or two sections. Problems later in the report are left to the rule-based methods.

---

## 16. The Combined Reliability Score

**In one line:** the 9 method scores are blended into one number from 0 to 1, shown as the score ring on the report.

### The formula (`compute_reliability`)

```
Reliability =
    0.13 × Factual Grounding        (Method 1)
  + 0.13 × Clinical Hard Rules      (Method 2)
  + 0.09 × SHAP Coherence           (Method 3)
  + 0.09 × Response Structure       (Method 4)
  + 0.08 × Severity Calibration     (Method 5)
  + 0.05 × Contraindication Safety  (Method 6)
  + 0.08 × Numeric Accuracy         (Method 7)
  + 0.13 × G-Eval                   (Method 8)
  + 0.22 × RAGAS Faithfulness       (Method 9)
  ───────────────────────────────
    1.00
```
The rule-based methods carry 65% of the weight and the AI judge 35%.

### What the score means

| Score | Label | Colour | What the clinician should do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0.75 or more** | High Reliability | green | Passed the checks. Use with normal clinical oversight. |
| **0.55 – <0.75** | Moderate Reliability | amber | Some gaps. Read the listed problems before acting. |
| **below 0.55** | Low Reliability | red | Several failures. Check every recommendation independently. |

Separately, a **caution flag** is set when the score is below **0.60**.

### Full worked example

```
Patient: SpO₂ 86%, MAP 61, HR 112, RR 22, SOFA 9.4, Stress 4, GCS Eye 4
Top SHAP features: SpO2_mean, latest_MAP, latest_HR

Report mentions oxygen, hypotension, heart rate and breathing, calls the
situation "significant", recommends oxygen and fluids, has all 4 sections
(310 words), and quotes "SpO₂ 86%" and "MAP 61 mmHg".

Method 1  Factual Grounding   SpO₂ ✓ MAP ✓ HR ✓ RR ✓ severity ✓ → 5/5   = 1.00
Method 2  Hard Rules          rules 1 and 2 apply, both met     → 1 − 0/2 = 1.00
Method 3  SHAP Coherence      3 of 3 features addressed         →        1.00
Method 4  Structure           4/4 × 0.7 + 1.0 × 0.3             →        1.00
Method 5  Severity            SOFA 9.4 (Moderate) → any tone OK  →        1.00
Method 6  Contraindication    no forbidden drug                 →        1.00
Method 7  Numeric Accuracy    86 and 61 match real values       →        1.00
Method 8  G-Eval              judge gives 4, 4, 4, 3 → (3.75−1)/4 = 0.69
Method 9  RAGAS               judge finds 1 unsupported claim   → 1 − 0.30 = 0.70

Reliability = 0.13(1.00) + 0.13(1.00) + 0.09(1.00) + 0.09(1.00) + 0.08(1.00)
            + 0.05(1.00) + 0.08(1.00) + 0.13(0.69) + 0.22(0.70)
            = 0.65 + 0.0897 + 0.154
            = 0.89  → High Reliability ✅
```

**The same patient with a bad report**, "Patient stable, continue monitoring" (60 words, no headings), with the judge giving 2, 1, 1, 1 and finding 2 unsupported claims:
```
M1 0.20 · M2 0.00 · M3 0.00 · M4 0.09 · M5 1.00 · M6 1.00 · M7 1.00 · M8 0.06 · M9 0.40
Reliability ≈ 0.03 + 0 + 0 + 0.008 + 0.08 + 0.05 + 0.08 + 0.008 + 0.088
            ≈ 0.34  → Low Reliability ❌, with the broken rules listed
```

### What the clinician sees

- **On the report:** the score ring with the label (High, Moderate or Low).
- **In the top patient bar:** the same "AI Reliability" percentage, always in sync with the ring.
- **In the LLM Validation drawer** (eye-icon button): the per-method breakdown and every specific problem found (broken rules, dangerous drugs, wrong numbers, unsupported claims).

### Cost per report

**2 AI calls per report** (1 to write, 1 to judge). The dashboard also prepares the other care mode's report in the background, so switching Hospital ↔ Home Care is instant. That makes about **4 calls per new reading**.

## 17. Known Limitations

Saying clearly what a system **cannot** do is part of validating it.

| # | Limitation | Effect | Possible fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | No lab data (blood gases, platelets, bilirubin, creatinine) | SOFA is estimated from indirect clues; precision inside a band is weak | Connect to the hospital lab system |
| 2 | The model under-predicts severe patients (average prediction for truly High Risk ≈ 9.7) | 33% of truly High Risk patients (198 of 602) get no alert, even at threshold 7 | Real lab inputs; simply weighting severe cases more in the loss was tried and did not help |
| 3 | The conformal q̂ is measured on training data | Real coverage on new patients is probably a little under 90% | Hold back a separate calibration set |
| 4 | One row per ICU stay, split 80/20 by ICU stay, not by patient | A patient with several stays can appear in both training and test data, which can flatter the scores. These are retrospective MIMIC-III results, not independent clinical validation | Split by patient ID; validate prospectively on new data |
| 5 | The judge model also wrote the report | The judge may rate the report too kindly | Use a different model as the judge |
| 6 | The judge sees only the first 900 characters | Later sections are checked only by the rule-based methods | Send the full report |
| 7 | Keyword methods (1–5) check for words, not meaning | "No need for oxygen" contains "oxygen" and would pass Rule 1 | Rely more on the judge, or detect negations |
| 8 | Numeric Accuracy compares a number with any real vital, not its own | A wrong MAP can pass if it is close to another vital | Match each number to its named vital |
| 9 | GCS Eye and Stress were mostly empty in the training data | Live camera values reach the model but barely change the SOFA | Retrain with properly filled values |

## 19. Panel Q&A

### Q: How do you validate the SOFA prediction?
**Three layers.**
1. **Offline:** on 10,450 ICU stays the model never saw, MAE = 1.82, R² = 0.47, AUROC = 0.925 and PR-AUC = 0.646, plus two confusion matrices (3 bands, and the alert). The SOFA Validation drawer reads these live from the training metadata.
2. **At runtime:** on every prediction, three checks flag anything physically implausible: a minimum SOFA from MAP, SpO₂ and GCS; dangerous vital combinations; and unexplained jumps.
3. **Uncertainty:** a conformal range of about ±3.34 points around each prediction, so the clinician can see how sure the model is.

### Q: How do you validate the AI report?
With **9 methods**. Seven are instant rules that check the report against the real vitals and ICU guidelines: mentions abnormal vitals, recommends the standard treatment, agrees with the model's main factors, has all sections, uses the right tone, avoids dangerous drugs, and quotes correct numbers. Two use an AI judge that grades quality and lists unsupported claims. They combine into one 0–1 reliability score with a list of specific problems.

### Q: Why is the High Risk recall only 67%?
The model under-predicts severe cases (their average prediction is about 9.7), mainly because SOFA depends on lab values and drug doses that our non-invasive inputs do not include. That is why the alert fires at a predicted 7 instead of 10, which raises how many sick patients are caught from 47.7% to 67.1%, while 96.3% of non-High patients still get no false alarm. The remaining misses are why the system supports doctors instead of replacing them.

### Q: Why is R² negative inside the risk bands?
Inside a narrow band, the differences come mostly from blood-test organs (clotting, liver, kidneys) that we cannot measure. The model places patients in the right band, which keeps the overall R² at 0.47, but it cannot reliably rank them within a band.

### Q: What if the reliability score is high but the report is still wrong?
That can happen, and we say so openly. To reduce the risk:
- the score is mostly rule-based checks against real data, not the model's own opinion;
- every specific problem is listed, not just the number;
- the SOFA alert fires independently of the report;
- every report carries a disclaimer that clinical decisions need a qualified professional.

### Q: Why 9 methods instead of one?
Each one catches a mistake the others miss:

| Method | Catches |
|---|---|
| Factual Grounding | an abnormal vital never mentioned |
| Hard Rules | the problem mentioned, but the standard treatment missing |
| SHAP Coherence | the report explaining something different from what drove the prediction |
| Structure | missing sections or a report that is too short |
| Severity | the wrong tone for how sick the patient is |
| Contraindication | a dangerous drug for these vitals |
| Numeric Accuracy | an invented number |
| G-Eval | quality problems keyword rules cannot see |
| RAGAS | individual false statements |

### Q: Which AI model is used?
`openai/gpt-oss-120b` via Groq, for both writing and judging. The SOFA number itself comes from our own federated neural network, not from the LLM.

## 20. Glossary

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **SOFA** | A 0–24 score of how badly six organs are failing |
| **MAP** | Mean arterial pressure: average blood pressure. Below 65 is dangerous |
| **SpO₂** | Blood oxygen level from a finger clip. Normal is 95–100% |
| **GCS Eye** | Eye-opening part of the Glasgow Coma Scale: 4 = opens by itself, 1 = never opens |
| **Held-out test set** | Patients kept aside and never used for training, used only for the final check |
| **MAE** | The average size of the prediction error |
| **R²** | How much better than "guess the average for everyone" the model is (1 = perfect) |
| **AUROC** | The chance the model ranks a sick patient above a non-sick one |
| **Precision** | Of the patients we flagged, the share who were truly sick |
| **Recall / sensitivity** | Of the truly sick patients, the share we flagged |
| **Specificity** | Of the patients who were not sick, the share we correctly left unflagged |
| **PR-AUC** | Combined precision and recall across all cut-offs; good for rare conditions |
| **Confusion matrix** | A table of predicted vs. actual categories |
| **Conformal interval** | A range built from past errors so it contains the truth about 90% of the time |
| **q̂ (q-hat)** | The error size that 90% of past patients stayed within |
| **SHAP** | A method showing which inputs pushed a prediction up or down |
| **LLM** | Large language model, an AI that writes text |
| **Hallucination** | AI text that sounds right but is not supported by the data |
| **Deterministic check** | A fixed rule that always gives the same answer for the same input |
| **LLM-as-judge** | Using an AI model to grade another AI's output |
| **Federated learning** | Training across several hospitals without moving patient data between them |
